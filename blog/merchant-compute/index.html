Field notes · Compute markets

The Contract
Is the Collateral

August 2026|Prannay Hebbar

You cannot rent this year's GPU by the hour. Not from anyone, not at any price. The chips exist and the demand exists. What's missing is a loan structure that allows it.

Here is how a GPU cloud gets built in 2026. An operator who wants to deploy $200M of hardware does not raise $200M. It signs a customer to a two-or-three year contract first, then borrows against that contract. A lender advances most of the capex, the chips go up, and the customer's monthly payments service the debt. The loan is underwritten on the customer's credit, not the operator's. In the biggest deals the rating agencies say this out loud: the facility is investment grade because the offtaker is investment grade.

The structure is borrowed from project finance. Power plants, pipelines, and cell towers all got built this way: prove the revenue with a signed contract, then fund the asset against it. For a datacenter shell it works fine. The problem is what it does when the asset is the chip.

Follow the consequence. Every new-generation chip is committed before it powers on. Whatever the lender financed stays locked to its contract for the full term. So the merchant market in current-generation compute is not thin. It is zero. If you want one node of the newest silicon for a week, the answer everywhere on earth is the same: come back with a three-year commitment and eight figures. The whole supply is spoken for by the financing that bought it.

The operator in this structure is barely a business. It is a courier between a lender and a customer that holds the depreciation risk and keeps the spread. Its growth is capped by how many creditworthy multi-year customers it can sign, which is why every pitch in this industry is a gigawatt story: the model only scales by finding bigger contracts.

Part one · The fragile book

01Correlated by construction

Now look at who signed those contracts. Mostly AI companies burning venture money, all exposed to the same cycle. A lender's book of these contracts looks diversified on paper: different names, different terms, different vintages. It is not. In a contraction, the customers downsize together, want the newer chip together, and renegotiate or default together. A book of correlated promises is one promise, made many times.

The industry has started admitting this in public, structurally if not verbally. Chip vendors now backstop the leases of their customers' customers, take warrants for guaranteeing demand for their own product, and rent back idle capacity at floor prices. When the vendor has to guarantee the demand so lenders will finance buying its chips, the contracts alone are not holding the structure up. The guarantee is load-bearing.

And the collateral underneath moved fast. The hourly price of the last flagship GPU fell from around $8 to under $2 in about two years as supply caught up. Fleets that were financed at the top of that curve still owe debt service priced at the top of that curve. The lesson lenders took was not "avoid GPUs." It was "the contract is the only thing protecting us," which made the presell requirement stricter.

Part two · The book nobody will finance

02The merchant model

There is another way to run a GPU cloud: sell by the minute. No contracts, no lock-in, hundreds of small customers instead of three big ones. In a shortage, on-demand pricing carries a premium over discounted term deals, so the merchant operator earns more per chip-hour than the contracted one. In a downturn it degrades gracefully: the price flexes, utilization holds, and nobody defaults because nobody signed anything.

A few operators run this model and run it well. One built a profitable on-demand cloud for a decade before taking its first outside dollar, at a $3.5 billion price. A nonprofit assembled one of the larger H100 fleets in the country and rents it by the hour, no contracts. I spent the last month talking to the people who run these clouds, and the strangest case is the smallest: a two-person shop running an AMD fleet at 100% utilization, customers queued for capacity, profitable, zero debt. It cannot raise a dollar of institutional money.

The safer book is the one that can't get financed. Venture capital passes because a cash-flowing hardware business is not a power-law bet. Infrastructure credit passes because there is no contract to lend against. The merchant operator sits in the gap between two underwriting traditions, and the gap prices it at zero.

The financing system does not lend against revenue. It lends against promises of revenue, and it cannot tell a promise from a good business.

Part three · The rails arriving

03We ran this experiment in power

Electricity markets solved exactly this, starting in the late 1990s. A power plant used to be financeable only with a long-term purchase agreement signed by a utility: same structure, same logic, the contract was the collateral. Then deregulation created merchant plants selling into hourly markets, and for a while they were unfinanceable too. What fixed it was not braver lenders. It was instruments: published price indices, exchange-traded futures, and revenue puts that let a merchant plant hedge its floor. Once the revenue could be hedged, "quasi-merchant" became a financeable category. The instrument replaced the contract.

Compute is speed-running the sequence. GPU rental price indices now publish on financial terminals. Exchange-listed rental futures begin trading this October. Residual-value insurance exists for clusters as small as 500 GPUs, guaranteeing a resale floor on the hardware itself. Non-recourse credit ring-fenced per cluster exists at the $30 to $500 million scale, where a default costs the lender one pod instead of the company.

Each rail replaces one thing the multi-year contract used to prove. The futures curve proves the price. The insurance proves the salvage value. The utilization history proves the demand. Stack them and the presell requirement stops being load-bearing. What remains of it is habit.

04The mispricing has an expiry date

Put the pieces together. The contracted book is correlated, backstopped by its own vendors, and priced as if the contracts were diversification. The merchant book is diversified, profitable at small scale, and priced at zero because neither underwriting tradition can read it. That gap is a mispricing, and the instruments that close it are already scheduled.

When merchant compute revenue can be hedged on an exchange, the merchant discount goes away, the same way it went away for power plants. The capital that learns to underwrite diversified hourly cash flow before the futures curve gets liquid buys the safer book at the fragile book's price. In power, that repricing took a decade. In compute, the futures list in six weeks.

Notes
1Offtake: the buyer side of a long-term capacity contract. Project finance rates the deal on the offtaker's credit, not the operator's.
2Advance rate: the share of the asset's cost a lender will fund. GPU deals run roughly 50 to 80%, higher with a contract or insurance attached.
3Merchant: selling into the open market at the going rate instead of under a long-term contract. Borrowed from power markets.
4Backstop: a third party guaranteeing a customer's obligations so a lender will fund the deal. In compute, that third party is increasingly the chip vendor itself.
5Revenue put: an option that pays out if market revenue falls below an agreed floor. The instrument that made merchant power plants bankable.
6Residual-value insurance: a policy guaranteeing a minimum resale price for the hardware at a future date, which caps the lender's downside without any customer contract.
